# Worriers May Find Doing Nothing Distressing, Says Study

When was the last time you were really idle? That means doing absolutely nothing — and with smartphones and being almost always online, true idleness is becoming rare.

Doing nothing might sound relaxing, but new research has found that people who are prone to worrying might actually find it distressing.

The research was published in _Cognitive Therapy and Research_. It used three studies with a total of about 900 participants to look at the relationship between worrying and what the researchers called "idleness distress."

The first study used an online questionnaire to ask almost 600 participants about their anxiety levels and how they felt about being idle.

The second study also used a questionnaire, this time asking 180 participants about boredom.

These participants were also told to sit in a room with nothing to do for 12 minutes — they could not get up, look at their phones, or even sleep. After the sitting task, they again reported how they felt.

The third study used about 170 participants, who again answered questionnaires on their general mood and mental health, and were again given 12 minutes to sit in a room.

This time, however, instead of having nothing to do, the participants watched part of a nature documentary chosen for being emotionally neutral — making their time in the room less idle than in the second study, but still not very exciting.

The participants in the third study were also asked how they expected to feel before the sitting task, and how they felt afterward.

The researchers found that across all three studies, participants who reported having a higher tendency to worry found being completely or even mostly idle more distressing.

The researchers said this may be because periods of idleness, of which we have fewer and fewer, are challenging because they direct our attention to our inner thoughts and feelings. For people who tend to worry a lot, this could make anxiety worse.
